\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Price charts are among the oldest tools for summarizing market history. Their familiar appearance can obscure a consequential data transformation. Before a statistical model sees a chart, each price path has been placed on a local scale, several variables have been aligned within a fixed window, and continuous observations have been converted into a spatial object. A convolutional neural network can extract strong return forecasts from this object \parencite{reimagining_price_trends_jiang}. The resulting portfolio performance, however, does not reveal whether the model has learned a visual pattern or has benefited from the way the chart reorganizes recent price history.

This distinction changes the economic interpretation of chart-based prediction. A visual mechanism would imply that the two-dimensional arrangement of bars, lines, and pixels contains useful structure that is difficult to recover from numerical observations. A representation mechanism would instead place the source of predictability in transformations made before the image reaches the network. Local high--low scaling is a leading candidate. It maps every stock-window into its own coordinate system, removes nominal price levels, and expresses daily movements relative to the recent range. A temporal learner can then compare paths across stocks without first estimating invariance to price level and scale. The image may provide an effective delivery format even when the underlying signal is a locally normalized time series.

We separate these channels by holding the recent market history fixed and varying its representation and learner. The chart convolutional neural network receives binary images of open, high, low, close, moving-average, and volume histories. Numerical models receive the same variables in chronological order. Logistic classifiers compare local high--low scaling with cumulative-return and volatility-based scaling before flexible feature extraction is introduced. A one-dimensional convolutional neural network then applies temporal filters to the locally scaled numerical sequence. Finally, a multimodal network combines the chart and sequence branches. This sequence of tests asks whether local scaling creates a useful ranking, whether temporal learning closes the gap to the chart model, and whether the two representations contain complementary information.

The design follows a simple empirical logic. If the picture contains distinctive predictive structure, a numerical sequence that retains the chart's scale should leave meaningful performance behind, and a model that observes both representations should benefit from their combination. If local normalization and temporal structure account for the main result, the locally scaled sequence should reproduce the chart portfolio and fusion should add little. The comparison cannot assign every residual difference to spatial layout. The image is binary and two-dimensional, the sequence is continuous and one-dimensional, and their networks differ in filter geometry and capacity. It can nevertheless establish whether a pixel image is required to recover the economically important ranking.

We estimate the models using U.S. common stocks from CRSP. Model development covers 1993--2000, and all portfolio evidence is evaluated out of sample from 2001 through 2024. Inputs and future-return horizons span 5, 20, or 60 trading days. At each formation date, stocks are sorted by the predicted probability of a positive return and assigned to deciles. We evaluate equal- and value-weighted high-minus-low portfolios at non-overlapping weekly, monthly, and quarterly frequencies. Common portfolio rules allow the model comparisons to reflect their rankings. Exact score-overlap tests use the common stock-date sample, while standalone portfolios use each signal's available cross-section.

The main finding is that chart construction exposes the signal before the chart CNN begins to learn. A logistic model produces little weekly portfolio performance from cumulative-return-scaled inputs and a strong ranking from the chart's local scale. Applying temporal convolution to the locally scaled sequence then reproduces the chart model's portfolio performance across all three weekly input windows. Local normalization creates the economically important coordinate system, and temporal learning extracts the remaining broad short-horizon structure.

The overlap evidence supports this interpretation. Chart and sequence scores produce substantially similar stock rankings, and their high-minus-low portfolio returns have correlations above 0.9 in the representative case. Joint cross-sectional regressions leave selected conditional associations, so rasterization and spatial filters can still influence individual predictions. Those residual differences rarely improve the traded portfolio. Feature concatenation and late fusion remain close to the stronger individual branch. The two encodings therefore recover a common locally normalized temporal component, with limited economically useful complementarity.

We next compare the learned rankings with transparent strategies based on the same broad information source. Medium-horizon continuation, one-month reversal, one-week reversal, and a rolling multi-horizon trend forecast establish that recent prices contain profitable structure outside the neural networks. The learned models produce a sharper weekly standalone ordering under the same portfolio conventions, while their advantage contracts at longer horizons and under value weighting. The benchmark comparison supplies an economic reference point for the representation result without redefining the paper as a horse race among trading strategies.

The economic assessment changes once the cross-section is restricted to more tradable stocks. The representative learned models retain strong full-universe net performance under a size-dependent transaction-cost schedule, while value-weighted portfolios are weak or negative after costs. A modest dollar-volume requirement makes every representative learned portfolio negative, as do the NYSE-microcap exclusion and stricter combined screen. The locally normalized signal is therefore strongest in the small and illiquid tail. This location is consistent with evidence that weekly reversal is concentrated among small, illiquid, and retail-dominated stocks \parencite{chenMaxingOutReversals2025} and illustrates how trading frictions can change the assessment of a return anomaly \parencite{novyMarxTaxonomyAnomalies2016}.

The return evidence also weakens in the final five years of the sample. Chart, sequence, and multimodal models decline together, as do one-week reversal and the rolling trend forecast. The common movement points to attenuation in the underlying short-horizon environment instead of a failure specific to image architecture. This later-sample result confirms an economic boundary on the shared signal and remains secondary to the representation mechanism. It mirrors the deterioration documented in a direct replication of chart-CNN portfolios \parencite{kaczmarekErodingNonScalableCNN2025} and fits the broader evidence that published return predictors weaken out of sample and after discovery \parencite{mcleanAcademicResearchDestroy2016}.

\subsection{Related literature}\label{subsec:related_literature}

Our paper relates first to research on technical analysis and return predictability from price histories. \textcite{loFoundationsTechnicalAnalysis2000} formalize visual chart patterns through systematic statistical recognition. \textcite{reimagining_price_trends_jiang} replace prespecified patterns with a CNN trained directly on chart images. \textcite{murrayChartingMachines2024} use machine learning on numerical histories of monthly returns and find nonlinear predictive content distinct from standard momentum and reversal, including among the largest 500 stocks. The chart-CNN replication of \textcite{kaczmarekErodingNonScalableCNN2025} places durability, microcap concentration, and implementation at the center of the image-based evidence. We study a different margin. Our tests hold recent price and volume histories fixed and ask which transformations of those histories explain the chart model's ranking.

The second connection is to machine learning in empirical asset pricing. Flexible methods can capture nonlinear interactions that linear models miss \parencite{guEmpiricalAssetPricing2020}, while recent evidence shows that carefully specified regressions can remain competitive in stock-return prediction \parencite{chengMachineLearningNecessity2025}. Our results connect these findings through input representation. Local scaling creates a strong linear ordering, and temporal convolution adds most consistently at the weekly horizon. Model complexity and data construction consequently enter the prediction problem jointly.

The third connection is to the economic evaluation of return predictors. Trading costs can reverse conclusions drawn from gross anomaly returns \parencite{novyMarxTaxonomyAnomalies2016}, and predictor performance commonly declines outside the discovery sample \parencite{mcleanAcademicResearchDestroy2016}. The concentration of weekly reversal among small and illiquid stocks provides a particularly close comparison for our short-horizon portfolios \parencite{chenMaxingOutReversals2025}. We combine these implementation tests with representation diagnostics, so the evidence distinguishes what the models learn from where the resulting portfolio can be traded.

Against this background, we make three contributions. First, we identify local high--low scaling as a concrete source of chart-CNN performance and show that a continuous temporal model can reproduce the central weekly portfolio. Second, we connect representation choice to signal substitutability through rank correlations, encompassing regressions, and multimodal fusion. Third, we map the signal's economic scope using transparent price rules, transaction-cost estimates, weighting schemes, liquidity screens, factor regressions, and a post-2019 evaluation. Together, these tests identify the representation mechanism and place strict limits on its tradable scope.
